# Scriptwriting Principles — Knowledge Base

Distilled from 4 tutorials on viral YouTube scriptwriting. Written as actionable rules for an AI agent
that scripts 45-50 second YouTube Shorts (English, US audience, Vox-style explainer animation).
Sections 1-5 are the source material's rules (long-form context); Section 6 adapts them to Shorts.

---

## 1. Hooks (the first seconds)

### Core job of the hook
- The intro decides whether a video gets 100 views or 100K — the biggest retention drop-off happens in the first seconds. Minimize that drop and the whole video's retention lifts.
- A hook has exactly two jobs: **click confirmation** and **curiosity gap**. Everything else is optional.

### Click confirmation (meet expectations)
- The title/thumbnail (packaging) creates an expectation in the viewer's mind. Confirm within the first 3-6 seconds that they'll get exactly what they clicked for.
- Confirm both ways: **say it** (first sentence closely echoes the title's promise) and **show it** (first shot visually matches the thumbnail).
- The viewer's brain runs on pain/reward: clicking was the effort; click confirmation is the reward. Deliver it or they scroll.
- You don't need a 100% literal match to title/thumbnail — just enough assurance that the promise is being kept.
- Never over-promise in the hook. If you do promise big, over-deliver in the body.

### Curiosity gap (a reason to stay)
- Plant a question the viewer cannot answer without watching to the end. "Warm them up, then leave them hanging."
- Tease the payoff without revealing it: preview the steps but blur/withhold them; show the best moment and cut just before the climax.
- Stack multiple curiosity gaps when possible — each teased item is a separate reason to keep watching.
- Proven curiosity-gap generators:
  - **FOMO** — imply they'll miss something valuable if they leave early.
  - **Relatability** — open with a situation the viewer has personally lived through.
  - **Fear / stakes** — a statement that signals possible personal loss.
  - **Question** — ask something the audience genuinely doesn't know the answer to.
  - **Pattern interruption** — state the opposite of the audience's normal belief ("Everything you've been taught about X is wrong").

### Context (why this matters)
- After confirming the click, give just enough context for the viewer to understand why the topic matters (the difficulty, the emotional weight, the real-world importance).
- Build context from the target audience's problems: list what the topic solves for them, compress into one sentence.
- Do NOT front-load all context — an intro that explains everything makes the rest boring and the intro too long.

### Differentiator (why watch YOUR video)
- Other videos exist on this topic. Give one signal that sets yours apart:
  - **Credibility/experience** ("I've read every book on this," "I've done this at scale").
  - **Effort/investment shown** — the brain values things that visibly cost time, money, or work.
  - **Unique angle, personality, storytelling, or visual style.**

### Assembly rules
- The order of context / curiosity / differentiator is flexible — connect them so they flow as one mini-story, not a checklist.
- Not every hook needs every element. Use what the topic demands.
- Stuck? Imagine yourself as the viewer: what would you need to see and hear in the first seconds to stay? Write that.
- Threat framing out-hooks opportunity framing: "If you don't do X, you'll never get Y" grabs harder than "Do X to get Y" — the brain prioritizes threats over opportunities.

## 2. Structure & storytelling

### Why story at all
- A story is just information presented engagingly: a character, a problem, a journey, a transformation. It has progression, tension, conflict, emotion, and mistakes — that's why storybooks are fun and textbooks aren't.
- Without conflict, stakes, and change, a video is "just a tutorial with tips." With story, watching becomes an experience, not an effort.

### Define the transformation first
- Before writing, state explicitly: where does the subject (or the viewer) stand at the START, and where at the END. A clear transformation arc is the spine of the script.
- Identify both the surface problem AND the deeper (often emotional) problem underneath it. Set up the deeper problem early; resolve it at the end.

### Hero's journey (full framework, condensed)
- Status quo → call to adventure (a problem, opportunity, threat, or curiosity) → mentor/insight → journey begins → trials (the main content: attempts, mistakes, wins) → the deeper problem surfaces → crisis (lowest point) → identity change / realization → reward (the lesson) → result → return → new life (changed mindset) → resolution.
- The crisis is where you show vulnerability — admit what you didn't know. Viewers see themselves in you; that builds loyalty.
- Don't apply it as a blind cycle; adapt it to the content.

### Sequencing information
- Research first: consume widely, note what existing sources explain AND what they don't — the gap is your opportunity.
- Turn notes into a logical outline: what comes after what, how pieces connect. Only then write.
- **But/Therefore rule (South Park):** never chain points with "and then... and then..." — that's linear and predictable. Chain them with "this happens, BUT this happens, THEREFORE this happens." Every beat should turn or consequence into the next.
- **Domino effect:** end each point by raising a question, limitation, or problem that the NEXT point answers. Points bridge naturally; a viewer hooked on point 1 is automatically hooked into point 2. Never let a new point feel random.
- **Retention dance:** alternate unique/surprising information with familiar information — lead with your best unique insight (earn trust that the video contains something new), then a known point, then unique again. The viewer can never find a "safe" moment to leave.
- **Paragraph framing:** structure each explanatory block as context → detail → challenge/twist → conclusion. Set the scene, deliver the substance, introduce the tension, resolve it.
- **Open loops & payoffs:** plant a concept early ("remember this for the end"), invite the viewer to figure it out before you reveal it, and pay it off at the close. Scatter small payoffs throughout, not just one at the end.
- **Sub-hooks (transitions):** attention fades every few minutes; re-hook at each section boundary. Formula: break the viewer's expectation ("but this alone won't work"), give the reason why, then hand them the next point as the solution.
- **Outro loop:** end by (1) summarizing the key points so the viewer leaves with them memorized, and (2) creating a need that the next video fulfills. Never end with a bare "like and subscribe."
- **Shock value (research quality bar):** value = the distance between what you know and what the audience knows. Widen it by consuming more/varied sources than the viewer would, by doing the thing yourself (experience they can't google), or by analyzing many examples into a format nobody else has assembled.

## 3. Retention tactics

- **Complexity ramp:** never open with advanced concepts — it sounds smart but loses viewers instantly. Give context for newcomers, explain the simple version, then gradually increase difficulty. Zero to 100 immediately = drop-off.
- **Pattern interruption for pacing:** a script in one continuous tone and flow bores the audience. Insert the unexpected — a joke inside an intense explanation, a sudden example, a tonal shift. Pacing controls emotion; if the script doesn't move the viewer's emotions up and down, it's dead.
- **Interactivity:** don't lecture — converse. Ask the viewer questions, give hints, and let them figure answers out before you reveal them ("See if you can spot it before I say it"). The self-earned "aha" feels good and forces attention.
- **Curiosity gaps everywhere,** not just the hook — every teased, blurred, or withheld payoff is a reason to keep watching.
- **Stakes:** give the viewer a reason to care by naming what's at risk. Ask "what happens if the viewer ignores this?" and say it ("skip this and X breaks"). Stakes = something to lose.
  - Caution: don't manufacture fear in every line — the audience desensitizes and long-term trust dies.
- **Relatability:** make the viewer the protagonist. Replace abstract statements ("background noise is a common problem") with lived micro-scenes ("you record a perfect 30-minute take, put on headphones, and hear the fan, the traffic, the dishes..."). The viewer stops consuming information and starts remembering their own experience.
- **Visuals carry the story:** every line should have the best possible matching visual. Mismatched b-roll or long static talking-head/one-visual stretches lose interest. Write visual direction into the script (editing notes per line).
- **Sound design:** music sets the emotional mood; sound effects punctuate beats (a price appearing → cash register). It's the salt in the food — unnoticed when present, glaring when missing. Match music mood to scene mood.
- **Compression:** after the draft, read the whole script start to finish and cut every line the story doesn't need. Fewer lines, smoother flow, higher retention.
- Accept the trade-off consciously: maximum retention favors only-simple content; if depth is the value, keep the valuable complexity and pay a small retention cost — but never keep filler.

## 4. Language & voice

- **Vary sentence length — monotone rhythm kills retention.** Beginners write same-length sentences over and over; mix short punches with longer flowing lines. Don't overthink it; just never let all sentences match.
- Write "but/therefore" connectives, not "and then" — at sentence level too, not just structure level.
- Write conversationally: questions to the viewer, direct address ("you"), rhetorical setups and reveals — a dialogue, not a monologue.
- **Label key concepts:** give your core idea a memorable name ("the Domino Effect," not "connecting points"; Apple says "AirDrop," not "wireless file transfer"). A label creates curiosity and makes the idea feel premium and ownable.
  - Restraint: label for emphasis, not decoration. If everything is special, nothing is. Don't rename already-clear or trivially basic concepts — it reads as forced complexity.
- **Analogies:** explain what they don't know using what they do know (impressions/CTR = 1000 people walking past a shop, 100 walking in). Analogies make concepts understandable, interesting, and memorable.
  - Analogies also set perspective: "YouTube growth is a lottery" implies luck; "YouTube growth is compound interest" implies patience and gradual gains. Choose the analogy that frames the mindset you want.
- Freewrite the first draft: timer on, no grammar/wording/structure worries, just expand each outline point onto the page. Edit afterward — reorder, add, remove, tighten flow.
- **Never let AI write the script from the outline — it produces generic writing no matter how much context it gets.** Use AI (or a person) only as a critic: "roast this outline — what's missing, what's boring, what should move, what about the twist?"
- Keep the soul: don't delete genuinely useful content just because it dents retention metrics, don't add rage-bait endings, don't manipulate. Technically-better-retention writing that loses honesty loses the audience.
- Show, don't tell — but go further: don't reveal, let the viewer deduce (see Interactivity).

## 5. What NOT to do

- Don't write the script before the packaging (idea, title, thumbnail concept) is fixed — a killer script with broken packaging is trash; the script must be written to serve the promise.
- Don't break expectations: if the content doesn't deliver what title/thumbnail promised, the video flops and trust erodes.
- Don't open with advanced concepts to sound smart — viewers leave.
- Don't front-load all context in the intro — it makes the intro long and the body boring.
- Don't over-promise (unless you knowingly over-deliver).
- Don't chain points linearly ("this happens, then this happens") — too predictable.
- Don't drop random topic jumps — every transition needs a bridge (domino effect / sub-hook).
- Don't write same-length sentences throughout — monotone rhythm.
- Don't keep one tone/flow for the whole script — no emotional movement means dead script.
- Don't lecture start-to-finish revealing everything up front — no participation, no attention.
- Don't use b-roll/graphics that don't match the line being spoken; don't sit on one static visual too long.
- Don't skip sound design — its absence is the first thing people feel.
- Don't label everything dramatically — forced labels on basic concepts destroy the effect.
- Don't spam fear/warnings in every line — audience desensitizes, trust collapses.
- Don't let AI write the script — generic output guaranteed; AI critiques, humans write.
- Don't optimize retention at the cost of value or honesty (no rage-bait, no cutting useful parts purely for the chart).
- Don't end on a bare like-and-subscribe beg — end on a summary plus a created need.
- Don't skip research because "I already know this" — there's always a gap between sources, and that gap is the video's edge.
- Don't publish the first draft — always do the compression pass.

## 6. Shorts adaptation notes (45-50s, Vox-style explainer)

Written for this project; maps the long-form rules above to a 45-50 second Short.

### What transfers directly
- Click confirmation → becomes **frame-one confirmation**: the first visual + first line must instantly confirm the topic the viewer's feed promised. No greetings, no "in this video," no channel intro — first word is already the hook.
- Curiosity gap, stakes, pattern interruption, but/therefore chaining, relatability, analogies, labeling, visual-per-line writing, sentence-rhythm variation, and the compression pass all apply at full strength — they just operate per-second instead of per-minute.
- The paragraph frame (context → detail → twist → resolution) is a perfect skeleton for one entire Short.

### What must be adapted
- **Hook in ~1.5 seconds, not 30.** One sentence, max two, doing double duty: confirm the topic AND open the curiosity gap. Best patterns for a Short: pattern-interrupt claim ("Everything you know about X is wrong"), an unanswered question, or a stakes line. Skip the differentiator — in Shorts, the hook itself and the visual style ARE the differentiator.
- **One single idea per Short.** No multi-point body, no sub-hooks between sections — there are no sections. The whole Short is one domino chain: hook → context (1 sentence max) → 2-4 but/therefore beats → payoff. If the outline has two ideas, make two Shorts.
- **One open loop, closed at the end.** Plant the question in the hook, pay it off in the final 5 seconds. The payoff should land as late as possible without feeling withheld — it's the retention engine for rewatches and completion rate.
- **Retention dance compresses to "no wasted beat":** every sentence must be either new information or a turn. Cut all throat-clearing, recaps, and transitions ("so," "now," "let's talk about"). Target roughly 110-150 spoken words for 45-50s.
- **Complexity ramp still applies but is steeper:** one sentence of grounding context, then straight to the interesting part. Use one strong analogy instead of a gradual explanation ladder.
- **Ending:** no summary outro, no "watch the next video" loop trap. End on the payoff line itself — ideally one that reframes the hook (loop closure encourages rewatch) or lands the "so what" for the viewer. A final line that loops cleanly back to the opening frame boosts replays.
- **Visuals:** Vox-style means the animation IS the explanation — script and visual must be written together. Write an explicit visual beat for every sentence; never write a line that can't be drawn. Sound design still matters: one music mood for the whole Short, SFX on key reveals.

### What to drop entirely
- Sponsor segments, channel lore, audience-avatar exposition, hero's-journey full arc (keep only its core: a tension introduced and transformed), multi-video "grand story" universe building, 30-second intros/outros, sub-hook transitions, like/subscribe requests mid-script.

### Quick checklist for every Short script
1. First 1.5s: topic confirmed + question opened?
2. Exactly one idea?
3. Every sentence a new fact or a turn (but/therefore), zero filler?
4. Sentence lengths varied? Reads aloud in under 50s?
5. Stakes or relatable moment present ("why should I care")?
6. One analogy or label doing heavy lifting?
7. Payoff in final seconds that closes the loop opened in the hook?
8. A drawable visual beat specified for every line?
